# Creative Selection

Load when the task includes competing concepts, demos, prototypes, or a request to choose a product direction.

## Purpose

Creative selection turns invention into a sequence of embodied alternatives and consequential rejections.

```text
not: brainstorm -> vote -> average everything
but: build -> experience -> compare -> reject/recombine -> commit -> expose to reality
```

## 1. Normalize candidates

Before comparing, describe each candidate with the same fields:

- initial user and situation;
- human before-state and after-state;
- core product object;
- default action or ritual;
- defining experience specimen;
- system boundary;
- adoption wedge;
- strongest failure mode;
- decisive uncertainty.

Do not compare a working prototype against a slogan or a mature incumbent against an imagined perfect product without naming the asymmetry.

## 2. Check structural diversity

Candidates must differ in product logic, not merely feature count, layout, or branding.

High-signal dimensions:

```text
object | actor | default | intelligence/control | boundary | cadence | wedge | operating model
```

If candidates are not distinct, return to generation. Selection cannot manufacture insight from duplicates.

## 3. Make the decisive experience concrete

Each serious candidate needs a specimen that shows:

- the trigger situation;
- the first 30 to 90 seconds;
- the moment the user obtains the new capability;
- system work hidden from the user;
- work and responsibility remaining with the user;
- the likely point of failure.

The specimen should answer “what happens?” before “what features exist?”

## 4. Experience before explanation

Where possible:

1. let reviewers use or watch the specimen without a pitch;
2. record their first interpretation, hesitation, action, and emotional response;
3. only then explain the intended thesis;
4. compare intended and perceived product models.

A concept requiring a long preamble may still be valuable, but the adoption burden is now explicit.

For each important pair, complete:


Do not force a winner when the concepts depend on different unresolved facts. Instead name the cheapest discriminating specimen.

## 6. Ask the maker

The directly responsible maker should provide a recommendation, not merely present options.

Ask:

- Which candidate do you believe should ship or advance?
- Which detail created that judgment?
- What did repeated making reveal that the review cannot see?
- What evidence would make you reverse the recommendation?

This uses local tacit knowledge without granting immunity from challenge.

## 7. Attack the winner

Before commitment, articulate the strongest version of the case against the leading concept:

- What if the old category exists for a good reason?
- What hidden work makes the magic moment possible?
- Which user has enough motivation to cross the learning or trust gap?
- Does the concept require ecosystem behavior the team cannot control?
- Is the discontinuity real, or merely a better demo?
- Could a narrower product deliver most value with less operating burden?
- What future constraint does the concept create?

A winner that survives only weak objections has not been tested.

## 8. Select, recombine, or stop

Allowed decisions:

```text
select A
select B
recombine a named mechanism from B into A
run a discriminating specimen before selection
reject all candidates and return to insight generation
stop because the opportunity is not strong enough
```

Forbidden pseudo-decisions:

- “combine the best of all three” without a coherent new product logic;
- “keep every option open” with no next test;
- select by stakeholder rank or presentation polish;
- invent a weighted score to conceal unresolved values.

## 9. Record rejection

For every serious rejected concept, keep:

```yaml
rejection:
  concept: ""
  why_it_lost: ""
  evidence_or_judgment: ""
  reusable_mechanism: ""
  revisit_condition: ""
```

Rejection history prevents the same idea from returning under a new label and preserves useful mechanisms without preserving whole concepts.

## 11. Roles

For consequential conception, distinguish:

- **maker**: builds and lives with the specimen;
- **product editor**: protects the whole-product thesis;
- **domain expert**: exposes constraints and false assumptions;
- **fresh user/reviewer**: reveals what the product communicates without context;
- **decision owner**: commits resources and accepts the consequence;
- **recorder**: preserves decision, confidence, rejected alternatives, and later outcome.

One person may hold several roles. Do not let every participant acquire veto power.

## 12. User evidence

Use users to discover:

- current behavior and workarounds;
- language and mental models;
- moments of confusion, trust, relief, or desire;
- whether the specimen communicates and changes behavior;
- what responsibility users refuse to delegate;
- where the proposed value is not valuable enough.

Do not require users to articulate the final discontinuous solution. Do not use that limitation as permission to ignore them.

## 13. Time and quality gates

Selection should balance two failure modes:

```text
ship too early -> judge a broken implementation rather than the concept
polish too long -> spend heavily before the decisive assumption is tested
```

Define:

- minimum fidelity needed for a fair experience;
- the assumption the specimen tests;
- maximum time or cost before review;
- conditions to continue, reframe, or kill;
- what quality is thesis-critical versus decorative.
